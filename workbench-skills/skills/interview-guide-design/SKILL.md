# Interview guide design

An interview guide for a discovery phase is not a survey. It exists to get
a small number of senior, time-poor people to say what they actually think,
in a single sitting each, in a way that a consulting team can later compare
and synthesize across interviews.

## Structure

For a guide covering several stakeholders (for example a CEO, a CFO, a COO
and a CIO), build it as:

1. **A short shared preamble.** One paragraph on the purpose of the
   interviews and how the notes will be used (synthesis into themes, not
   attributed quotes in a public document, unless told otherwise). This
   keeps every interviewer consistent on framing, even if several people on
   the engagement team run different interviews.
2. **One section per stakeholder**, each with:
   - A **1-2 question warm-up**: low-stakes, easy to answer, gets the
     conversation started (their role, what's top of mind this quarter).
   - **4-8 core questions**, specific to that person's remit and stake in
     the outcome — not the same list repeated with the name changed. A
     CFO's questions are about funding, risk and financial exposure; a
     COO's are about operational continuity and delivery risk; a CIO's are
     about the landscape they own and the decisions in front of them. Pull
     the specifics from the engagement context and the stakeholder notes:
     a generic question ("what are your priorities?") is a wasted minute
     with someone who is only in the room for forty-five.
   - A **closing question**: "what haven't we asked that we should have?"
     or equivalent. This is where genuinely unexpected material tends to
     surface.
   - A note on **estimated time** for that section.
3. **A suggested running order and total time budget** across all
   interviews, so whoever schedules them knows what they're asking for.

## What good looks like

- Questions are **open-ended**: they invite the interviewee to describe a
  situation or a view, not to confirm or deny something the interviewer
  already believes.
- Questions surface **pain points, ambitions and constraints** — not a
  technology wish-list. "What's broken and what does it cost you?" beats
  "would you like a new ERP?"
- Each stakeholder's section reflects something **specific to them**: a
  detail from the engagement context or the stakeholder notes that shows
  the guide was written for this person, not templated.
- Where the brief signals a genuine ambiguity about a stakeholder (for
  example: someone new in their role, where it's unclear whether to probe
  their forward-looking view or the history they inherited), the guide
  should make a **deliberate choice** about where that section's limited
  time goes, rather than splitting it thinly across both and getting
  neither.
- The guide is usable by someone who didn't write it: a colleague running
  one of the interviews should be able to pick it up and know what to ask
  and why it matters.

## Pitfalls

- **One list, four names.** Reusing the same questions across stakeholders
  because it's faster to write wastes the interview and produces notes
  that don't triangulate on anything.
- **Leading questions.** "Don't you think a cloud ERP would solve this?"
  tells the interviewee the answer you want instead of finding out what
  they think.
- **Going technical too soon.** Executives generally think in terms of
  risk, cost, timeline and relationships, not system architecture. Start
  there; let technical detail come up if they raise it.
- **No closing question.** The most useful thing in an interview is
  sometimes the thing nobody thought to ask about — the closing question
  is what catches it.
- **Ignoring known tensions.** If the context or stakeholder notes already
  hint that two people are likely to disagree (for example on urgency, or
  on how much change the organization can absorb), the guide should ask
  each of them directly rather than avoiding the topic — synthesis depends
  on having both sides on the record.
